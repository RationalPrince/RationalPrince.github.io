Desk reference

The Strait of Hormuz, answered properly.

The most-asked questions about the world's most important oil chokepoint — with the part most coverage leaves out: what each answer actually does to price.

Where is the Strait of Hormuz, and who controls it?

It connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea. Iran sits on the northern shore; Oman and the UAE sit on the southern shore, with the shipping lanes running through Omani territorial waters at the narrowest point.

No single state owns it. Transit passage through an international strait is governed by the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, and the United States Fifth Fleet, based in Bahrain, operates in the region. Control is therefore contested in practice rather than settled in law — which is precisely why it prices risk.

How wide is the Strait of Hormuz?

Roughly 21 miles (about 33 km) at its narrowest. The usable shipping lanes are far tighter than that: two lanes of about two miles each, separated by a buffer, which is why the chokepoint is a genuine bottleneck rather than a wide sea lane.

The width matters for one reason. It puts every transiting cargo inside range of shore-based systems on the northern coast, and it means disruption does not require closure — it only requires enough uncertainty to move insurance.

Why is the Strait of Hormuz important to oil markets?

Around a fifth of global petroleum liquids consumption transits it, along with a substantial share of seaborne LNG. Saudi Arabia, Iraq, the UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, and Iran all export through it. No other chokepoint concentrates that much supply in that little water.

The market impact is rarely about the barrels themselves. It shows up first in war-risk insurance premiums, then in freight rates, then in the shape of the crude curve as buyers pay up for prompt cargoes — usually before any physical shortfall exists.

What would happen if the Strait of Hormuz closed?

Closure has been threatened many times. The question that matters for positioning is not whether transit stops but for how long, and how much the bypass routes absorb.

Two pipelines exist to route around it: Saudi Arabia's East–West line to the Red Sea and the UAE's Habshan–Fujairah line to the Gulf of Oman. Their combined spare capacity covers only a fraction of normal transit volumes, and Kuwait, Qatar, and Iraq's southern exports have no bypass at all. A short interruption is a freight and insurance event. A sustained one is a supply event — a distinction that decides whether the trade is days long or months long.

The full Strait of Hormuz reference covers the bypass routes in detail, and the Playbook maps the escalation tiers, the bypass capacity line by line, and where each tier shows up in instruments first.

Is the Strait of Hormuz open right now?

This page is a reference, not a live tracker — check current shipping advisories for real-time status. What the desk publishes instead is the framework: the observable triggers that precede a disruption, and the sequence in which markets reprice once one appears.

Waiting for a headline to confirm status is, structurally, the losing side of this trade. The move is usually in insurance and freight before it is in the news.

Which sovereign wealth funds and defense names does this touch?

The Gulf sovereign wealth funds — Saudi PIF, ADIA, Kuwait's KIA, QIA — are funded by exactly the barrels transiting this water, so chokepoint risk and sovereign allocation are the same story told at two speeds. Norway's Norges Bank is the mirror: an oil-funded allocator hedging away from oil.

On the defense side, naval presence, minesweeping, air defense, and munitions restocking are the procurement lines that move on Gulf escalation — which is why the desk covers defense stocks and energy under one thesis rather than two.
