Sovereign capital, in plain terms.

04 / Field Notes

What is a sovereign wealth fund?

A sovereign wealth fund is a state-owned investment fund. A government takes money it does not need to spend today — oil and gas royalties, trade surpluses, or central bank reserves — and invests it in assets abroad: listed equities, bonds, real estate, infrastructure, and private companies.

The purpose is intergenerational. A commodity exporter is converting a depleting asset in the ground into a permanent financial one, so the income outlives the resource. That mandate is why these funds behave differently from hedge funds or pensions: the holding period is measured in decades, and the flows are steady rather than tactical.

Which country has the biggest sovereign wealth fund?

Norway. The Government Pension Fund Global — the "oil fund," managed by Norges Bank Investment Management — is the largest single sovereign wealth fund in the world, valued at roughly $2.3 trillion as of mid-2026, with around 72% of that in equities.

Its scale is the point: the fund holds on average about 1.5% of every listed company on earth. When Norway rebalances, it is not expressing a view — it is mechanically moving size across global markets. That is why the fund's allocation shifts are worth reading even if you never trade a Norwegian asset.

Which countries have sovereign wealth funds?

Roughly a hundred funds exist across some 60 countries. The ones that move markets cluster in two groups.

Commodity exporters: Norway (GPFG), Saudi Arabia (PIF), the UAE (ADIA, Mubadala, ADQ), Qatar (QIA), Kuwait (KIA), Russia (NWF), and Oman. Surplus and reserve funds: China (CIC), Singapore (GIC and Temasek), and South Korea (KIC). Several U.S. states run their own — Alaska's Permanent Fund and the Texas Permanent School Fund being the largest.

Does the United States have a sovereign wealth fund?

Not at the federal level, historically. Unlike Norway or Saudi Arabia, the U.S. runs deficits rather than surpluses, and Congress has generally directed federal resource royalties toward specific appropriations rather than a permanent savings vehicle.

That may change. Executive Order 14196, signed February 2025, directed Treasury and Commerce to produce a plan for a U.S. sovereign wealth fund covering funding mechanisms, structure, and governance. Standing one up at scale would likely require legislation. Watch the funding mechanism above all — where the capital comes from determines what the fund is actually allowed to buy, and therefore which sectors would absorb the flow.

How do sovereign wealth funds invest?

Most run a long-horizon, index-anchored core — global equities and fixed income — with a satellite sleeve for real estate, infrastructure, and direct private stakes. Norway sits at the transparent, near-passive end. The Gulf funds sit at the other: PIF and Mubadala use capital as industrial policy, buying stakes that build domestic capability, not just returns.

For an allocator, the useful distinction is mandate. A stabilization fund sells into strength to plug a budget hole. A savings fund buys weakness and holds. Knowing which type is on the other side of a large print tells you whether the flow is likely to continue.

Why do sovereign wealth funds matter for energy and commodity markets?

Because the funding source and the market are the same trade. When crude runs hot, exporters accumulate surplus and sovereign funds deploy it — into equities, infrastructure, and increasingly into energy transition and data-centre capacity. When crude breaks, the same governments draw down to cover fiscal deficits, and the fund becomes a seller.

That makes sovereign flows a lagged, leveraged echo of the oil price — and a leading indicator for which sectors get bid next. Tracking the barrel tells you what the sovereign will do six to twelve months out. Tracking the sovereign tells you where the capital lands.
