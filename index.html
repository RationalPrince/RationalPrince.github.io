Oil price forecasts: how they are built, and how to read one

Every bank publishes a crude oil price forecast. Almost none of them are right, and that is not the interesting part. What matters is the assumption stack underneath the number, because that is the thing you can actually check.

What a forecast is actually made of

A crude oil price forecast is not a prediction in the way a weather forecast is. It is a balance calculation with a price attached at the end. The forecaster estimates demand, estimates supply, works out whether inventories build or draw across the period, and then attaches the price level that historically corresponds to that inventory path.

That means every published number rests on three inputs, and only three really matter:

The three inputs behind any crude forecast
InputWhat it estimatesReliability
Demand growthGlobal consumption, mostly driven by Asian industrial activityModerate
OPEC+ policyWhether the group adds, holds or cuts productionLow — it is a political decision
Non-OPEC supplyUS shale, Brazil, Guyana and the restModerate, with long lead times

A fourth input — disruption risk at chokepoints like the Strait of Hormuz — is usually excluded from the base case and carried as a scenario instead.

Why the horizon changes everything

The single most useful question about any forecast is how far out it goes, because the honesty of the exercise collapses as the horizon shortens.

Next day or next week. Dominated by inventory surprises, positioning and headlines. None of those are forecastable, and the research literature is blunt about it: very few published short-horizon forecasts beat the assumption that tomorrow's price equals today's. If someone is selling you a next-week crude target, they are selling confidence rather than analysis.

Next quarter. Balance arithmetic starts to bite. Inventory direction becomes visible in weekly data, and a forecast can be checked against it as the quarter progresses. This is the shortest horizon where a forecast is worth reading.

One to three years. Supply lead times dominate. Projects sanctioned today land in this window regardless of sentiment, which makes the supply side genuinely estimable. Demand is the weak leg.

A forecast you cannot check is not research. The value is in the assumptions, because assumptions can be falsified and numbers cannot.

Why the banks disagree

When Goldman, Morgan Stanley and the EIA publish different crude targets for the same year, the gap is almost never a disagreement about method. They all run the same balance calculation. The gap comes from small differences in the three inputs, compounding.

A half-million barrel a day difference in assumed demand growth — well within normal estimation error — flips a year from surplus to deficit. The headline price target moves by ten dollars or more on the back of it. This is why quoting a bank's number without its assumption table tells you almost nothing.

The practical consequence: when you see a forecast revised, look at which input moved. A revision driven by an OPEC+ decision is a different animal from one driven by a demand markdown, and they imply different things about what happens next.

How to read a forecast critically

  • Find the assumption table first. If it is not published, the forecast is a marketing document. Agency forecasts publish theirs; the EIA Short-Term Energy Outlook is the accessible benchmark.
  • Check which benchmark it prices. A Brent forecast and a WTI forecast are not interchangeable — see why the spread between them moves.
  • Note whether disruption risk is in the base case. Usually it is not, which means the published number implicitly assumes nothing goes wrong at any chokepoint.
  • Track the revision, not the level. The direction and driver of a change carry more information than the target itself.
  • Ignore anything with a next-week price target. The horizon is shorter than the method can support.

Common questions

How accurate are crude oil price forecasts?

Poor at short horizons, mixed at long ones. Few published forecasts beat the assumption that tomorrow's price equals today's. Read them for their assumptions rather than their numbers.

Can anyone forecast the oil price for next week?

Not reliably. At that horizon crude is driven by inventory surprises, positioning flows and headlines, none of which can be forecast. A next-week target is confidence sold as analysis.

Why do bank oil price forecasts disagree?

They use the same method with different assumptions about demand growth, OPEC+ policy and non-OPEC supply. Small input differences compound into large differences in the headline number.

What is the EIA oil price forecast?

The US Energy Information Administration's monthly Short-Term Energy Outlook publishes crude projections alongside the supply, demand and inventory assumptions behind them. Because the workings are public, it is a better reference point than a bank target quoted without context.
