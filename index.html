Is the Strait of Hormuz closed?

The questions people are actually searching, answered with the part most coverage leaves out: what each answer does to price.

Is the Strait of Hormuz closed right now?

Effectively yes, and it has been since late February 2026, when the United States and Israel began strikes on Iran. Iran has enforced the closure by attacking vessels attempting transit, and the United States has run a naval blockade alongside an escort operation.

Closed does not mean empty. Traffic has become conditional rather than absent — some vessels move by permit, some under escort, some dark with tracking disabled. Weekly transit counts have run at a fraction of pre-war levels, recovering and collapsing again with each round of attacks.

This page is a reference, not a live tracker. For current status check maritime advisories; for the framework that tells you what a change in status is worth, see the Playbook.

Why is the Strait of Hormuz closed?

The closure is Iran's response to the war that began in late February 2026. It is leverage rather than an end in itself — the strait is the one asset Iran holds that imposes costs on every party at once, including its own buyers.

In May 2026 Iran went further and established a Persian Gulf Strait Authority, asserting that no vessel may pass without a permit it issues. That claim runs against the geographic fact that the strait comprises both Iranian and Omani territorial waters. A memorandum in June briefly lifted crossings before attacks in late June and early July collapsed it.

Who controls the Strait of Hormuz?

No single state owns it. Iran holds the northern shore; Oman and the UAE hold the south, with the shipping lanes running through Omani territorial waters at the narrowest point. Because the strait links two areas of high seas, transit passage applies under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea — a right that cannot lawfully be suspended. Iran has never ratified UNCLOS, which is the legal ambiguity every escalation cycle reaches.

The practical answer is that control is contested rather than settled, which is exactly why the water carries a permanent risk premium.

How wide is the Strait of Hormuz?

About 21 miles — roughly 33 kilometres — at its narrowest. The navigable portion is far tighter: two lanes of about two miles each, separated by a buffer.

That width is why disruption never required a formal closure. Every transiting cargo passes within reach of shore-based systems on the northern coast, so credible threat alone is enough to move war-risk insurance and stop owners nominating vessels.

What happens to oil when the Strait of Hormuz closes?

Roughly twenty million barrels per day of oil and petroleum products transited before the war, alongside a large share of seaborne LNG. Brent has traded up substantially against pre-war levels, carrying a risk premium analysts have put at around a tenth of the price.

The repricing runs in a consistent order: war-risk insurance first, then freight and charter rates, then the front of the crude curve, then refined product cracks, with equities last and least informative. Waiting for a headline to confirm the story is structurally the losing side of this trade.

Can pipelines bypass the Strait of Hormuz?

Partly, and far less than the reassurance implies. Saudi Arabia's East–West pipeline runs to Yanbu on the Red Sea; the UAE's Habshan–Fujairah line reaches the Gulf of Oman below the chokepoint. The IEA has put their combined available capacity at roughly 3.5 to 5.5 million barrels per day — against about twenty million transiting before the war.

Coverage matters more than capacity. Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain and southern Iraq have no overland alternative at all, so a pipeline serving Saudi and Emirati barrels does nothing for Qatari LNG. Both terminal ends have also been attacked during this conflict. The UAE is accelerating a second line to roughly double Fujairah capacity, targeted for 2027.

What does the permit regime mean for reopening?

This is the question most coverage misses. Blockades end; administrative layers do not. If vessels eventually move freely but under permits, tolls and inspection, the waterway is open in the sense that cargo arrives and closed in the sense that transit is now a cost with a political counterparty.

So the word to watch in any settlement is not reopening but administration — whether an agreement names an authority, a fee, or an inspection right, and who holds it. Markets price reopening headlines quickly and administration terms slowly, and that gap is where the asymmetry sits.
