Crack Spread Explained: How Refining Margins Signal Oil Demand

Refining margins decide how much crude the world buys. Here is how to calculate the crack spread and what it tells you before price does.

By Rational PrinceNovember 9, 20268 min read

Crude oil has no use until it is refined. What refiners can sell gasoline, diesel and jet fuel for, compared with what they pay for crude, determines how hard they run their plants, and so how much crude the world buys. That margin is the crack spread, named after the cracking process that breaks heavy hydrocarbons into lighter fuels. It is one of the most useful demand signals in the oil market and one of the least watched outside professional desks.

What the crack spread measures

At its simplest, a crack spread is the price of a refined product minus the price of the crude used to make it, both expressed per barrel. A wide spread means refiners earn a good margin and have every reason to run at high rates. A narrow or negative spread means they lose money on each extra barrel and will cut runs, defer purchases or bring maintenance forward.

Because refiners are the buyers of crude, their margin feeds straight back into crude demand. Strong cracks pull crude into refineries. Weak cracks leave crude sitting in storage.

The 3-2-1 crack spread, step by step

A typical US refinery turns roughly three barrels of crude into about two barrels of gasoline and one barrel of distillate (diesel and heating oil). The industry benchmark copies that ratio:

3-2-1 crack = (2 × gasoline + 1 × diesel − 3 × crude) ÷ 3

The detail that trips people up is units. On NYMEX, WTI crude is quoted in dollars per barrel, but RBOB gasoline and ULSD (ultra-low sulfur diesel, traded under the old "heating oil" contract) are quoted in dollars per gallon. There are 42 gallons in a barrel, so product prices must be multiplied by 42 first.

Worked example (illustrative prices)

  • WTI crude: $70.00 per barrel
  • RBOB gasoline: $2.20 per gallon × 42 = $92.40 per barrel
  • ULSD: $2.50 per gallon × 42 = $105.00 per barrel

Products: (2 × 92.40) + 105.00 = $289.80. Crude: 3 × 70.00 = $210.00. Difference: $79.80. Divided by three barrels, the 3-2-1 crack is $26.60 per barrel.

These numbers are examples, not quotes. The method is what matters.

Single-product cracks

Desks also track each product on its own, because gasoline and diesel often move in opposite directions:

Gasoline crack

RBOB (×42) minus crude. Seasonal: it usually strengthens into the US summer driving season and the switch to summer-grade fuel, then fades in autumn.

Diesel (distillate) crack

ULSD (×42) minus crude. Driven by freight, farming, industry and winter heating. Diesel is the fuel of the real economy, so a falling diesel crack is often an early sign of slowing industrial activity.

European gasoil crack

ICE low-sulfur gasoil minus Brent. Gasoil is quoted per metric tonne, so it is converted using roughly 7.45 barrels per tonne. This is the main global read on diesel tightness outside the US.

What moves crack spreads

  • Refinery outages and turnarounds: planned maintenance in spring and autumn, plus unplanned outages, cut product supply and widen cracks.
  • Product inventories: weekly gasoline and distillate stock data are the direct check on margin strength.
  • Seasonal demand: summer driving for gasoline, winter heating and harvest for distillates.
  • Trade flows: sanctions, export bans and shipping disruptions change where products can go. Product markets often react faster than crude because product stocks are thinner.
  • Crude quality and price: a sharp crude rally with no matching move in products squeezes margins, and refiners respond by cutting runs.

Why investors should care

A demand signal for crude. Strong cracks mean refiners want more crude, which supports crude spreads and prices in the months ahead. Collapsing cracks often come before refinery run cuts and weaker crude buying.

A driver of refiner equities. Refining company earnings track crack spreads closely. A refiner's share price is largely a bet on future margins, with crude price a secondary factor.

A check on the headline. When crude rallies but cracks weaken, the rally is being driven by supply or financial flows, not end-user demand. That rally deserves less trust. When crude and cracks rise together, demand is real.

A chokepoint read. Gulf refineries export large volumes of diesel and jet fuel. Disruptions to Gulf shipping can lift product cracks in Europe and Asia even more sharply than crude. See the Strait of Hormuz desk reference for the current picture.

How the crack is traded and hedged

Refiners hedge by selling the crack forward: they lock in a margin by selling product futures and buying crude futures together. Traders take the other side through exchange-listed crack spread futures and options on CME, or by building the spread from separate crude and product futures. Because the crack is a spread, it removes much of the flat-price risk, which is why it is a popular trade during uncertain markets.

The bottom line

The crack spread is the market's live answer to one question: is anyone actually using the oil? Track the 3-2-1, then gasoline and diesel separately, then check product inventories. Margins usually turn before crude demand does. Pair it with the curve shape covered in contango vs backwardation and you have the two numbers most physical traders look at before they look at price. Frequently asked questions

What is a crack spread?

A crack spread is the difference between the price of refined products such as gasoline and diesel and the price of the crude oil used to make them, expressed per barrel. It approximates a refiner's gross margin.

How do you calculate the 3-2-1 crack spread?

Multiply gasoline and diesel futures prices (quoted per gallon) by 42 to convert to dollars per barrel. Then compute (2 × gasoline + 1 × diesel − 3 × crude) and divide by 3 to get the margin per barrel of crude.

What does a high crack spread mean?

A high crack spread means refiners earn strong margins, so they tend to run harder and buy more crude. It often reflects tight product inventories, refinery outages or strong seasonal demand.

Why do investors watch crack spreads?

Crack spreads drive refiner earnings and act as a demand signal for crude. When crude rallies but cracks weaken, end-user demand is not confirming the move.
